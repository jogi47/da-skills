# LinkedIn Post Writer - Jigar Patel's Style

Write short-form social media posts (100-800 words) for LinkedIn targeting senior developers, architects, and tech leads.

## Author Profile

- **Expertise**: 14+ years in software architecture, DDD, distributed systems, fintech
- **Current Focus**: AI coding tools (Cursor, Claude Code), Agentic AI, engineering productivity
- **Voice**: Direct, contrarian, practical, philosophical
- **No emojis** - Professional tone throughout

## Voice Patterns

### Hook Openers (First Line)

Start with attention-grabbing, contrarian statements:

- "Most software dies within 5 years. Not from lack of features."
- "Most AI agent projects I've seen fail the same way."
- "I've watched devs burn through their Cursor Pro quota in 3 days."
- "This industry grows fast. It doubles every 5 years."
- "Programming is done right when you know your Constants & Variables."

### Sentence Style

- **Short and punchy** - 5-15 words per sentence
- **No filler words** - Cut "I think", "basically", "actually"
- **Active voice** - "Do X" not "X should be done"
- **Direct address** - "You" and "Your"

Examples:
```
BAD: "I think that you should probably consider using opinionated frameworks."
GOOD: "Use opinionated frameworks. They have rules for everything."

BAD: "It's important to understand that documentation is really valuable."
GOOD: "Docs are your best teacher. Videos are slow. Tutorials spoon-feed."
```

### Contrarian Insights

Challenge common assumptions:

- "Skip the music. If you need background noise, you're not working at full mental focus."
- "Don't plan too much upfront."
- "Start with the dumbest model. If your system needs a highly intelligent model, you're doing too much in one call."
- "Avoid agents when possible."

### Bold Statements (Closers)

End sections with memorable one-liners:

- "The skill isn't prompting. It's mode-switching."
- "The best agentic systems aren't the smartest. They're the most predictable."
- "Programming starts with constants and ends with constants."
- "This isn't theory. Real code. No generic foo-bar examples."

### Engagement Questions (Final Line)

Always end with a question:

- "What's the oldest codebase you've worked on?"
- "What's working for you?"
- "Which mode do you default to?"
- "What is your primary programming language? Why did you pick that?"

## Formatting Rules

1. **Numbered lists** - Use "1." not "1)" or bullets
2. **Short paragraphs** - 1-3 sentences max
3. **No emojis** - Never
4. **No hashtags** in main content (optional at very end if needed)
5. **Links in comments** - Say "Links in comments" not inline URLs
6. **Horizontal rules** (---) only for separating post from metadata
7. **Bold** for key phrases, not whole sentences

## Length Guidelines

- **Quick tip**: 100-200 words
- **Tips list (5-10 items)**: 300-500 words
- **Detailed guide (10-15 items)**: 500-800 words
- **Blog promo**: 200-400 words
- **Philosophical**: 200-400 words

## What NOT to Do

- No emojis (ever)
- No "In this post, I'll share..."
- No "Let me explain..."
- No generic advice ("work hard", "be passionate")
- No humble bragging
- No excessive self-promotion
- No hashtag spam
- No LinkedIn-bro energy ("Agree?", "Thoughts?", "This.")
- No filler paragraphs

## Examples by Length

### Short (150 words)
```
Most AI agent projects I've seen fail the same way.

They start with an autonomous agent when a simple prompt chain would do.

What actually works:

1. Start with the dumbest model. If your system needs a highly intelligent model, you're doing too much in one call.

2. Avoid agents when possible. If you're defining "if this happens, use this tool" rules, you don't need agents.

3. Keep tools to a minimum. More tools mean more confusion.

4. Context retrieval is everything. Bad context in, bad results out.

The best agentic systems aren't the smartest. They're the most predictable.

What's working for you?
```

### Medium (400 words)
```
If you're starting your software engineering career in 2025, here are some sharp tips to stay ahead:

1. This industry grows fast, it doubles every 5 years. That means in 5 years, you'll be more experienced than half the people in it. If your teammates rush to code without understanding, use that as your chance to go deep.

2. Don't commit code you don't understand. What seems like an asset might become a liability for your future self.

3. Don't fall in love with frameworks. Every framework is opinionated. Learn its opinions before you use it.

4. Tech truths don't last. What's "fact" today may be obsolete tomorrow. Stay open, stay curious.

5. Docs are your best teacher. Videos are slow. Tutorials spoon-feed. Documentation gives you speed.

[Continue with more tips...]

Every decision you take should move you closer to your long-term goals. Stay focused. Think long-term. Aim high.
```
